Exposure

Guide · rendering · cameras · living document

Lights in this engine use physical units: the sun is about 100,000 lux, a lamp a few hundred lumens. Exposure turns those values into a picture, the way a camera's aperture, shutter and sensitivity do. Exposure belongs to the Camera component; a Post Process Volume can only offset it (the Exposure Adjustment effect, in stops). Views without a camera, such as the editor's Scene View, use the camera defaults.

Exposure modes

ModeWhat sets the exposureUse it for
Auto (default)The camera meters the scene every frame and adapts over time, like an eye.Games where the player moves between bright and dark places.
ManualManual EV, an absolute EV100: 15 is bright sun, 12 overcast, 7 to 9 an interior, 2 to 5 night.Shots that must not change with what is on screen.
PhysicalAperture, shutter time and ISO, as on a real camera (f/16, 1/100 s, ISO 100 is about EV 14.6).Matching photographic reference.
FixedA plain linear multiplier.Non-physical content.

Exposure Compensation applies in every mode, in stops: +1 doubles the brightness, -1 halves it.

How Auto metering works

Auto exposure looks at the pixels of the frame before bloom and tone mapping and sets the exposure so their average lands at 18 % grey, the middle grey of a photograph. Two rules keep it from being fooled:

  • The darkest and brightest tenth are ignored. Deep shadows and specular highlights do not move the meter.
  • The sky is not part of the average. The meter reads what the camera sees of the world: the ground, buildings and characters. Pixels where nothing was drawn (the sky, the sun, clouds) are counted apart. The sea is part of the world: an ocean surface is metered like the ground, so a coast, a ship or a flight over water is exposed for the water and what stands on it. A character standing against a bright sky is exposed for the character, not for the sky behind it.

The sky still has a say when it fills a large part of the frame. If its average would sit more than two stops over middle grey, it holds the exposure back so it does not burn to white. How much it holds back follows its share of the frame:

Sky in viewEffect on exposureExample
Under 10 %None: the scene is metered on its own and the sky may blow out into bloom.A dark room with a daylight window; turning toward the window brightens the meter through adaptation.
10 % to 30 %Grows with the share.A street with a strip of sky.
Over 30 %Full: the sky's average sits at most two stops over middle grey.A landscape at dusk; a model on a floor under the open sky.
95 % to 100 %The meter blends from the scene toward the sky as the scene shrinks below 5 % of the frame, and meters only the sky when nothing else is in view.Looking up into open sky from a cockpit.
What lands at middle grey is the average of the world in view (the middle 80 % of its brightness), not any one object. An 18 % grey card reads middle grey when the scene around it averages 18 % and the sky is not holding the exposure back. Two things move it away:
  • A bright sky over 30 % of the frame (dawn, dusk, a model under the open sky): the sky's limit holds the exposure back, and the ground sits about half a stop under middle grey.
  • A floor or ground lighter than 18 % (snow, sand, pale stone, a white studio floor): when the floor fills most of the view, the meter keys it to middle grey, so what stands on it reads darker by up to log2(albedo / 0.18) stops: typically up to about 1.7 for a floor of albedo 0.6 and 2.5 for a white (1.0) floor such as the Default Material. The less of the view the floor fills, the smaller the offset (a model on a white floor under the open sky measured 0.7 to 1 stop). Raise Exposure Compensation by the offset you see to put an 18 % subject back at middle grey.
Exposure Compensation is the only control that moves the target.

Troubleshooting

SymptomCauseFix
Everything reads dark in bright sun, and the ground is very lightThe meter keys the average to middle grey; light materials (snow, sand, pale grass) average brighter than 18 %.Check material base colors against real albedos (grass 0.1 to 0.25); or add positive Exposure Compensation.
